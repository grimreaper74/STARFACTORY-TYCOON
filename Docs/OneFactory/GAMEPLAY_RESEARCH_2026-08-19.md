## 1. THE CORE LOOP

The evidence converges on a single minute-to-minute loop across Factorio, Satisfactory, Production Line and the wider management genre:

**OBSERVE → DIAGNOSE → PAUSE → ADJUST → RESUME → SCALE → NEW BOTTLENECK**

Concretely, as Line Boss should implement it:

1. **Observe.** The player watches the plant run. This only works if motion is diagnostic — a starving station, a blocked conveyor, a body queuing before paint must be visually distinct states readable at a glance (Wube's stated design rule for belts vs pipes, FFF #430; "You can see the mistake. You can see the fix"). The watchable plant already built is the product, provided every station broadcasts starved / blocked / running / degraded visually.
2. **Diagnose.** The bottleneck is found by looking, not by opening a spreadsheet. HUD numbers confirm what the eye already suspected.
3. **Pause and adjust.** Pause is a play mode, not an interruption — TPH players report 80/20 paused-to-running ratios; CS players build whole networks paused. The player pauses, places or reroutes machines via the HUD, resumes.
4. **Resume and verify.** The fix is visible within seconds: the queue drains, flow resumes. Immediate visual confirmation closes the loop.
5. **Scale.** Solving one shop's bottleneck moves the binding constraint to another shop (press output starves weld; weld throughput floods paint). Constraints must only bite at scale (McClure's Factorio analysis), so growth continuously regenerates problems — "problems within problems", "no downtime".
6. **Unlock.** Progress is paid for in throughput (Satisfactory milestones: physical parts, not points) and paced deliberately — shapez targeted "something new every 15 minutes"; Wube tunes the research tree explicitly as the pacing backbone. Contracts are Line Boss's natural unlock currency: fulfil orders → earn money and reputation → unlock machines, robots, shop expansions.

The addiction is the cascade: each solved bottleneck creates the next, each unlock changes the maths, and the running plant continuously advertises what to do next. Session shape follows: long, absorbed, overrunning intent (Anno's built-in two-hour warning).

## 2. SIX DESIGN PILLARS

**Pillar 1 — The plant is the UI (maps to: watchable plant, WIP flow).**
Bottleneck legibility at a glance is a first-class design principle (FFF #430). Every station needs visually distinct starved/blocked/working states; bodies queuing before a slow station should be the primary diagnostic. Corollary from the CS2 "fake economy" scandal: the visible flow must be *truthful* — players actively probe whether bodies genuinely move press→weld→paint→assembly, and trust collapses if the spectacle is decorative. Line Boss's existing stage-to-stage body movement is exactly right; never fake it for performance.

**Pillar 2 — Cascading constraints across four shops (maps to: WIP flow, machine placement).**
Each shop's output must be another shop's binding constraint, and constraints should only appear at scale. Publish clean cycle-time numbers per machine (Satisfactory's multiples-of-60 lesson: clean numbers let players invent the ratio puzzle themselves — "That emphasis is made by the players"). Layout stays player-driven, not scripted: the crafty-buildy audience punishes fenced-in builders.

**Pillar 3 — Total economic transparency (maps to: money/orders HUD, contracts).**
Production Line's market died as "an excel sheet that doesn't really matter" because there was no per-car cost breakdown and no visible reason cars sold or didn't. Harris's own stated key lesson: players need transparent feedback on why cars sell. Line Boss must show per-car cost breakdown (materials + energy + robot wear + overhead per unit), per-contract margin, and cause-and-effect on every economic event. His marketing post-mortem generalises: mechanically functional systems fail if their effects aren't shown — every system Line Boss ships needs a visible feedback surface in the HUD.

**Pillar 4 — Robot condition as the maintenance rhythm (maps to: robot condition values).**
A lights-out plant has no workers, so robot wear is the humanising drama and the mid-loop tension. Production Line's late defects system (1.49) added a valued efficiency-vs-quality tradeoff; Line Boss can do it natively: worn robots run slower or produce defects, condition is visible on the machine itself (Pillar 1), and the player trades line uptime against maintenance windows. This also differentiates: no competitor stages modern robotic production as the spectacle — every existing title models workers or abstracts the floor.

**Pillar 5 — Contracts as the pacing backbone (maps to: contracts mockup).**
The unlock treadmill is the retention engine (shapez: new thing every 15 minutes; Satisfactory: unlock currency is throughput itself; Wube: progression must feel "deserved"). Escalating contracts — volume, spec, deadline, quality thresholds — are Line Boss's milestone system. Critically, they are also the answer to challenge collapse (CS players abandon saves ~2 hours in once income exceeds all costs; Production Line's late game collapsed when one line satisfied all demand): contracts must keep escalating so money and orders never stop mattering, and multi-line/multi-shift demand must exceed what one optimised line can serve.

**Pillar 6 — Pause is a first-class play mode (maps to: time controls).**
Full build/HUD interaction while paused, designed into the tick architecture *now* — Ubisoft told Anno players active pause "cannot be retrofitted" because the engine never stops. Wide speed range (players split 80/20 vs 20/80 pause ratios; Big Ambitions players resented a fixed clock). Paused actions should be cheap or free to undo (CS players argue paused bulldozing deserves 100% refund; Production Line's punishing layout mistakes discouraged experimentation). Optional gentle session-length nudge à la Anno.

## 3. WHAT TO AVOID

**Performance that degrades with success — the genre's #1 review killer.**
CS2 (4090 at 50 FPS), Techtonica (20–30 FPS bases), Anno megacities at 20 FPS. ONI shows per-agent pathfinding is the usual culprit (~80% of frame time) — directly relevant to Line Boss's robots and moving bodies. *Counter:* budget per-robot pathing/AI from day one; bodies on fixed conveyance paths, not free pathfinding; scope invalidation narrowly (Production Line's Manhattan-distance culling gave 2x — over-broad invalidation, not the algorithm, was the killer). Factorio and DSP prove optimisation becomes a *marketable virtue*.

**Late-game collapse / repetition.**
Production Line: one line met all demand, feature creep killed the price tiers, nothing left after the megafactory. Automation Empire: "same mission in different scenery". *Counter:* design the late game first — demand that requires multiple lines, per-tier viability, a completable goal (Satisfactory's Project Assembly finish line), contract variety so run 2 differs from run 1, and play-past-the-win.

**Onboarding failure.**
Captain of Industry's top negative theme (learning curve, 44 mentions); W&R's "12 hours on YouTube"; Rise of Industry's "no competent tutorial". Harris's post-mortem: tutorials cannot rescue non-self-evident UI — critical controls need persistent visual anchors at the thing they control. *Counter:* in-game tutorial covering the management loop (not just controls) plus self-evident HUD affordances.

**UI pain as a headline review topic.**
Planet Coaster 2's laggy convoluted UI drove its Mixed rating; Production Line's undismissable notifications and 4K-unreadable text drew negatives. *Counter:* treat HUD responsiveness and legibility as a reviewed feature; test at 4K.

**Save corruption.**
Satisfactory's lost-30-hours threads — it *will* happen in a big Unreal factory game. *Counter:* copy Satisfactory's mitigation: rolling backups of the last N saves plus load-time validation.

**Simulation dishonesty.**
CS2's ghost citizens became a betrayal narrative. *Counter:* the visible plant must be the simulation.

**Abandonment and monetising over complaints.**
Production Line's post-1.0 stop compounds "abandoned" reviews years later; Car Manufacture died of silence ("No pulse for half a year"); CS2's Beach Properties DLC hit 4% positive. *Counter:* budget post-release support; never ship paid content while known complaints stand.

**Unrecoverable death spirals.**
CoI's semi-soft-locks read as wasted hours. *Counter:* warnings before spirals, recoverable failure (e.g. emergency contracts/loans at reputational cost).

## 4. NAMING

**Recommendation: keep "Line Boss", subtitle "Car Factory Tycoon" — i.e. *Line Boss: Car Factory Tycoon* — with "Line Boss: Car Factory Simulator" as the fallback, decided by capsule/wishlist testing.**

Reasoning:
- The brand half is clean: zero Steam matches for "Line Boss"; the only existing Line Boss® mark is an industrial label applicator in a different goods class (low risk, but run formal USPTO/EUIPO clearance in games/software classes before committing — web search is not a clearance).
- The branded-name + literal-descriptor pattern is proven in this exact niche by *Automation – The Car Company Tycoon Game* (~16k reviews, 94/100). Name-SEO barely matters (Zukowski, 94k-game study); the subtitle's job is instant genre legibility, not search.
- Avoid "Simulator" as first choice: median gross for "Simulator" titles is ~$70k with extreme hit-skew, ~39 new ones per month, and the exact phrase "Car Factory Simulator" is already Appscraft's mobile product. "Tycoon" better matches the management fantasy and the Automation precedent — but note *Car Factory Tycoon* (Jomica) releases 29 Sept 2026 with a demo live; do not launch into its window, and a subtitle (not a bare title) collision is survivable.
- Discovery weight goes elsewhere anyway: the hook (lights-out all-robot plant, bodies visibly flowing press→weld→paint→assembly — unserved by every competitor), correct top-5 tags (Automation, Management, Simulation, Building, Automobile Sim), a logo readable at 120×45 px, and a demo launched well before any Next Fest (~2.5x wishlist yield vs launching during; 16.3% median demo-to-wishlist conversion).
- Market gap check: no title combines a watchable 3D plant + working management + sustained development. Production Line proved the loop reviews well (82%) and now idles at ~22 concurrents; every 3D successor failed on execution/abandonment, not concept. This audience punishes abandonment and jank harder than modest scope — a dense, finished, honest plant beats a broad shallow one.

## 5. OPEN DECISIONS FOR THE OWNER (max 5, with recommended defaults)

1. **Structure: sandbox spine with contract-driven goals, or scenario campaign?**
   *Default: sandbox spine.* Sandbox carries the genre's hours (CS: 12M copies, no campaign; only 14.3% finish Anno's campaign; TPH was forced to add sandbox in two months). Layer contract chains as optional goals on the one persistent plant — never reset a running, profitable factory (TPH's per-level resets churned reviewers by hour 15).

2. **Endgame shape: infinite escalation or a completable finish line?**
   *Default: both* — a "flagship contract" finish line à la Satisfactory's Project Assembly (its 1.0 praise centred on having one), with play-past-the-win escalation after. Pure infinity collapses (Production Line); pure finish lines end saves.

3. **Failure pressure: can the player go bankrupt?**
   *Default: soft failure.* Warnings before spirals, rescue mechanisms (emergency loans/contracts at reputational cost), no hard game-over in sandbox. Players configure pressure *out* (Anno players delete AI opponents); CoI's death spirals generate negative reviews. Offer a hardcore toggle for the tail.

4. **Defects/quality system: in scope for v1?**
   *Default: yes, minimal version* — robot condition already exists; wire worn robots to defect probability, add one inspection/rework station type, tie quality to contract acceptance. It is the cheapest route to Production Line's most-valued late addition and gives robot condition gameplay teeth rather than being a bare number.

5. **Name commitment timing.**
   *Default: commission USPTO/EUIPO clearance on "Line Boss" in games/software classes now (cheap, unblocks everything), defer the subtitle choice until capsule A/B testing near Steam-page launch, and schedule the page + demo well clear of Car Factory Tycoon's 29 Sept 2026 window.*

---

**One-line summary:** Line Boss's already-built watchable plant is, per the strongest developer evidence in the genre (Wube, FFF #430), the actual product — the work remaining is to make every visible motion diagnostic, every economic cause transparent in the HUD, pause a first-class build mode, contracts the pacing backbone that prevents challenge collapse, and per-robot simulation cost bounded from day one.

## production-line
Deep-dive on Production Line (Positech, Cliff Harris; Steam app 591370, EA May 2017, 1.0 March 2019, ~9,115 solo-dev hours). What players praised: the realism of watching a car factory actually work (reviews say processes 'resembled the reality of actual production facilities'), a clean UI, and a genuinely addictive optimization loop good for 50-60 hours. What made them quit, in order of evidence weight: (1) late-game collapse - one production line satisfies all market demand, feature creep kills the budget/mid tiers so everyone converges on luxury-only, and after the efficient megafactory exists there is nothing left to do; (2) a market sim that read as 'an excel sheet that doesn't really matter' - no per-car cost breakdown, opaque reasons why cars sell or don't; (3) punishing cost of layout mistakes discouraging experimentation; (4) mid-EA economy-balance whiplash (component prices up, car values down) that made runs feel impossible; (5) post-1.0 abandonment souring recent reviews. Harris's own dev-blog lessons map directly onto Line Boss's current state: research pacing breaks when facility cost becomes trivial (he favoured specialized labs to fix 'click-click-click' research spam, and rewarded first-to-research with temporary premium pricing); a market model needs segmentation, competitor pressure and above all *visible feedback* on why cars sell; the marketing system only started being used once campaign results were shown in modal dialogs with live metrics; critical controls (production scheduling) failed because they lived behind an unmarked button at the line-start slot - tutorials cannot rescue non-self-evident UI; a late defects/inspection/rework system added a valued efficiency-vs-quality tradeoff; and at ~9,000 slots, over-broad route invalidation, not the pathfinding itself, was the performance killer (Manhattan-distance culling = 2x speedup). Business side: ~13k Steam copies in 77 days plus 10k direct, press coverage weakly correlated with sales, Facebook ads dominant, no-discount pricing benchmarked against Factorio/RimWorld. Actionable for Line Boss: design the late game first (multi-line incentives, per-tier viability caps, endgame goals/scenarios), make every economic cause visible in the HUD (per-car cost breakdown, why-it-sold/why-it-didn't), gate research pacing against factory wealth, keep mistakes cheap to undo, and budget for post-release support since 'abandoned' reviews compound.

## factory-addiction
Research complete: 16 sourced claims from developer posts (Factorio Friday Facts #430, #275, #376), developer interviews (tobspr/GameDiscoverCo, Coffee Stain/GameRant, shapez 2/Testify), professional reviews (RPS, Thumb Culture), and player forums, on what mechanically makes Factorio, Satisfactory and shapez addictive. The converging findings: (1) VISIBLE FLOW IS THE PRODUCT — Wube designs so bottlenecks are readable 'at a glance' and treats opaque systems (pipes) as design failures; analyses confirm the running factory doubles as its own debugging UI ('You can see the mistake. You can see the fix'), directly validating Line Boss's watchable-plant investment, provided starvation/blockage states are visually distinct per station. (2) THE LOOP IS CASCADING BOTTLENECKS AT SCALE — every solved line creates upstream demand; constraints only bite at scale, forcing diagnose-redesign-expand cycles with no natural stopping point ('problems within problems', 'no downtime'). Line Boss needs each shop's output to become another shop's binding constraint. (3) UNLOCKS ARE THE PACING BACKBONE — Factorio tunes its research tree explicitly for pacing ('better paced and longer', progression that feels 'deserved'); Satisfactory charges milestones in physical parts so throughput is the unlock currency; shapez targeted 'something new every 15 minutes' via analytics. (4) RATIO PUZZLES ARE PLAYER-INVENTED FROM CLEAN NUMBERS — Satisfactory's multiples-of-60 rates let players self-impose perfect-ratio play; publish clean cycle-time numbers per machine and players build the puzzle themselves. (5) COMBAT/STRESS IS NOT THE DRAW — Coffee Stain shelved combat for traversal/efficiency/cosmetics; shapez succeeded by deleting stressors entirely, good news for a lights-out plant with no threats. (6) FIRST-HOUR FRICTION IS THE COMMERCIAL RISK — shapez 2's 25 playtests on the first 30-120 minutes preceded 150k sales/98% positive. No dedicated GDC talk on the factory genre was found in the GDC Vault; the strongest developer evidence is the Friday Facts posts and the tobspr and Coffee Stain interviews cited.
Claims:
- [high] Wube (Factorio's developer) states in Friday Facts #430 that transport belts are the fun core because they are 'extremely visual and interactive' — 'you can see exactly where items are coming in, where they are going, and where the bottlenecks are, all at a glance' — and contrasts them with pipes, which are 'almost entirely opaque' and therefore fail to give sufficient visual feedback. Their stated design rule: any limitation added to a system 'needs to be extremely easy, obvious, and interactive.' Bottleneck legibility at a glance is a first-class developer design principle, not an accident. (https://www.factorio.com/blog/post/fff-430)
- [medium] Watching a factory run is satisfying only when motion is diagnostic: 'You can see the mistake. You can see the fix.' A starving machine, a clogged belt, or a backed-up route visually tells the player what to repair, so the running plant doubles as its own debugging UI (examples given across Factorio, Satisfactory and shapez 2, where the system 'either moves correctly or it does not'). (https://gamefoundry.games/blog/factory-games-most-satisfying-automation)
- [high] Players on the Factorio Steam forum attribute the addiction to cascading 'problems within problems': every solved production line creates a new demand upstream, there is 'no downtime' (while waiting on one thing there are ten more that need doing), output targets mean 'you can't relax on achievements,' and research unlocks continuously open new building possibilities — producing endless progression with no natural stopping point, which players call 'the good kind of addiction' driven by real problem-solving rather than artificial engagement mechanics. (https://steamcommunity.com/app/427520/discussions/0/359543951719601561)
- [high] Engineers interviewed by LeadDev describe Factorio's pull as 'fun reward loops which involve some combination of mundane work, aesthetic creativity, spatial problem-solving, logic, and optimization' (David Wolever) and 'Factorio scratches the same itches that a lot of software engineering does. You get all the time you want to debug stuff, refactor stuff, and build new stuff' (Blake Winton); the game's nickname among fans is 'Cracktorio.' (https://leaddev.com/career-development/factorio-and-the-promise-of-better-software-engineering)
- [high] Erik McClure's widely-cited essay argues Factorio's core loop is scale-driven bottleneck engineering: constraints (like belt throughput limits) 'are almost never a problem until you reach a certain scale, at which point they begin to dominate your efficiency'; one stalled production line cascades failures up the dependency chain like a framework-level bug; and factories eventually outgrow spaghetti layouts, forcing refactoring to 'a more scalable framework.' The addiction is repeated diagnose-redesign-scale cycles, not one-off puzzles. (https://erikmcclure.com/blog/factorio-is-best-interview-we-have/)
- [high] shapez.io creator Tobias Springer told GameDiscoverCo he deliberately stripped Factorio's stressors — 'I really loved Factorio [but] I found it too stressful… I wanted to make a game which is as approachable as possible' — removing enemies, power management and resource scarcity so the entire game is 'the puzzle-centric optimization part.' Approachable, pressure-free optimization was itself the hook that sold ~$1M on Steam. (https://newsletter.gamediscover.co/p/deep-dive-how-shapezio-went-from)

## session-patterns
Evidence-backed picture of how management/tycoon players actually play, from Cities: Skylines, Two Point Hospital, Big Ambitions and Anno 1800. SESSION SHAPES: the norm is long, absorbed sessions that overrun intent — Anno 1800 literally ships a narrator warning at the two-hour mark and reviewers report not noticing it arrive; the moment-to-moment loop is observe-the-sim, pause, adjust, resume, with the pause/run ratio varying from 80/20 to 20/80 between players. PAUSE: pause is not an interruption feature, it is a play mode — Cities: Skylines players build entire networks paused and want paused actions treated as free planning; Anno's inability to build while paused is a years-long grievance Ubisoft says cannot be retrofitted because the engine never stops (design-lock warning for Line Boss's tick architecture); Two Point Hospital guides teach pause as the no-penalty pressure valve. SANDBOX VS SCENARIO: sandbox carries the hours — CS sold 12M+ copies with no campaign; only 14.3% of Anno players finish the campaign, which the community treats as a tutorial handing off into sandbox; TPH launched scenario-only and was forced to add sandbox within two months; but scenario goals have a real defense (devs: pure freedom converges on same-y builds) and even scenario fans demand play-past-the-win-condition. Players also self-tune pressure downward (disabling Anno AI opponents), so difficulty/pressure toggles matter. STOP SESSION VS STOP FOREVER: saves die from challenge collapse (CS: once income exceeds all costs ~2 hours in, players restart; only 5.7% ever reach 100k pop — the mid-game is where everyone lives); players quit forever from forced repetition of solved problems (TPH's per-level hospital resets churned reviewers by hour 15) and from update-added friction (a 500-hour Big Ambitions fan quit over nagging employee complaints, returning only after a patch). Implications for Line Boss: build active pause with full build/HUD placement while paused into the sim's core now; offer a wide speed range plus a gentle session-length reminder; make sandbox the spine with contract-style scenario goals layered on top rather than level resets; never take a running, profitable factory away from the player; and ensure money/orders keep mattering late via escalating contracts so challenge does not collapse.

## car-genre
The car-manufacturing sub-genre splits cleanly by which fantasy each game serves, and the evidence shows the factory-floor fantasy is validated but unserved. ## naming-market
Verdict: "Line Boss: Car Factory Simulator" is viable but the subtitle needs thought. The brand half is clean — no Steam game named or containing "Line Boss" exists (1,356 loose search results, zero exact matches), and the only existing "Line Boss(R)" trademark is Advantage Label & Packaging's industrial label-applicator machine, a different goods class from games (low conflict risk, but run a formal USPTO/EUIPO clearance in the software/games classes; web search found no games-class registration). The subtitle half is crowded: Steam already has Car Factory (2023), Epic Car Factory (2018), Car Factory Empire (2025), Car Factory Tycoon (releasing Sept 29, 2026 with a demo live now), and incumbent Production Line: Car factory simulation (Very Positive, ~2,159–3,449 reviews), while the exact phrase "Car Factory Simulator" is already used by Appscraft's mobile games on Google Play/App Store. Market evidence cuts both ways on literal naming: "Simulator"-titled games have a median gross of only ~$70k with extreme hit-skew (Supermarket Simulator $22.2M vs #2 at $3.5M) and ~39 new "Simulator" titles debuted in June 2025 alone; PlayWay's descriptive-title portfolio topped all publishers in top-wishlisted games (29 vs EA's 3); but Zukowski's 94k-game study found no title-length/sales correlation, name-SEO barely matters, only ~13% of games use colon descriptors (declining), and Car Manufacture's Mixed 66% shows a literal name can't carry weak execution. The branded+descriptor pattern is proven in this exact niche by Automation - The Car Company Tycoon Game (~16k reviews, 94/100). Practical implications: the name pattern is fine (consider "Line Boss: Car Factory Simulator" or "...Car Factory Tycoon" tested against each other; avoid shipping near Car Factory Tycoon's Sept 2026 launch window); genre demand is real (management/simulation in top-5 genres by 1,000+-review rate, blue-collar sims surging, "crafty buildy" audience loyal); wishlists will come from the hook (lights-out all-robot plant with visibly moving bodies is a differentiator vs every worker-management competitor) plus correct top-5 tags (Automation, Management, Simulation, Building, Automobile Sim), a logo-only capsule readable at 120x45px, and a demo launched well before any Next Fest (2.5x the wishlist yield vs during; median demo-to-wishlist conversion 16.3%; Next Fest multiplies existing wishlists rather than generating them). Design-side: crafty-buildy buyers punish fenced-in builders — keep plant layout player-driven.

## failure-modes
Researched why factory/tycoon games earn negative Steam reviews, across Cities: Skylines 2, Techtonica, Anno 1800, Oxygen Not Included, Factorio, Satisfactory, Production Line, Automation Empire, Captain of Industry, Workers & Resources, Rise of Industry, Planet Coaster 2, Good Company, Dyson Sphere Program and several small tycoons. Five recurring complaint patterns emerged, each with multiple corroborating sources. (1) Performance that degrades as the factory grows is the genre's number-one review killer: CS2's 4090-at-50FPS launch, Techtonica's 20-30 FPS bases, Anno 1800's 20 FPS megacities; ONI shows per-agent pathfinding is the usual culprit (~80% of frame time) — directly relevant to Line Boss's robots and moving bodies. Factorio and Dyson Sphere Program prove the converse: treating optimization as a design pillar becomes a marketable virtue. (2) Shallow endgame/repetition: Automation Empire's 'same mission in different scenery', Production Line's 'same setup every game', small tycoons 'done in 5 hours'; Satisfactory 1.0 shows a concrete finish-line goal fixes this. (3) Onboarding failure: W&R's 'you will spend 12 hours on YouTube', Captain of Industry's top negative theme (learning curve, 44 mentions), Rise of Industry's 'no competent tutorial' — deep sims survive complexity only when the tutorial does. (4) UI pain is a headline review topic in management sims (Planet Coaster 2's convoluted/laggy UI, CoI and W&R clunky controls, Good Company's broken logistics UI). (5) Save corruption: Satisfactory's lost-30-hours threads show it will happen in a big Unreal factory game; its rolling SaveGames_backup folder is the mitigation to copy. Two bonus patterns: players actively probe whether the simulation is real (CS2's 'fake economy' scandal — for a watchable-plant game, visible bodies must genuinely flow stage to stage or trust collapses), and monetizing before fixing complaints multiplies negativity (CS2 Beach Properties refunded at 4% positive). Design-against checklist for Line Boss: budget per-robot pathing/AI from day one and expose a performance-friendly build path; give the campaign a completable goal plus contract variety so run 2 differs from run 1; invest in an in-game tutorial that covers the management HUD loop, not just controls; treat the HUD's responsiveness as a reviewed feature; ship rolling save backups plus load-time validation; make the visible plant truthful to the simulation; and avoid unrecoverable death-spiral states without warnings.
